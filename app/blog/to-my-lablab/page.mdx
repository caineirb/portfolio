# I Love My Alone Time, But I Love You More: An Observation on the Restorative Effects of Romantic Companionship Compared to Solitude 

A love letter for Pauline May "Lablab" Coming Bautista

## 1.0 Introduction
For the longest time, I have been a *"someone"* who prefers to be alone, excluding myself in events that pushed me back from society at large and making me feel *"left out"*.I built walls larger than that of China just to satisfy my fear of being left out by other people. I wanted to be alone, that solitude was the only thing that comforts me. It was fun, I had routines, I had plans everyday inside the 4 corners of my room, and going outside was a nightmare. But in a twist of events, I meet **you**. You were the first person whom I didn't have plans to be involved with. You were the first person who made me think of *"Bahala na si Lord asa ni padulong"* and I'm not even a religious person when you met me. Talking to you, spending time with you, playing with you, and just casually chatting with you even after an exhausting day were the times I genuinely was confused whether *did I get a rest or nah?*

## 1.1 Background of the Study
I was so accustomed to wanting, needing time alone to rest, be consumed by my own thoughts, be in silence with the voices I can't even hear, then suddenly, we talked. You became something that I wanted to experience after a tiring day, you became the time I needed after isolation, you were the thought that I can be alone with comfortably, and you became that voice that made the others quite. At first, it was confusing, sure, I experienced similar feelings with my previous relationships, but with you, it was different. Before, that feeling was like drinking a cup of coffee just to finish what's needed to be done. Forced, exhausting, and dangerous. Always pursuing something that should be easily given.

Then there comes **you**. You were like a sunrise after a good night sleep. The morning breeze that soothes and energize my body allowing me to move all day long. It was mesmerizing yet uncomfortable at first. I keep asking myself, do I deserve a treatment like this? Are all of these real or just me gaslighting myself after all that happened to me? All of those questions that lingers in my mind each time we talked, then I grew tired of them and finally decided that I'm done undervaluing myself and just letting myself enjoy all of these. I want to experience this forever, I want **you**, and just **you**, forever. 

## 1.2 Statement of the Problem
Experiencing something new comes with an uncomfortable feeling and uncertainty. Constantly questioning reality if all of these are just temporary, worthwhile, or just a glimpse of an alternate world where everything went well for me. As someone who preferred his solitude more than anything else in the world, the researcher observed for 11 months&mdash;as of writing this paper&mdash; whether this new found feelings were objectively beneficial to oneself than solitude.

## 1.3 Research Objectives
The following were the general and specific objectives of the study.

### 1.3.1 General Objectives
The general objective of this study was to examine whether the *"lablab"* Pauline May Coming Bautista felt the same experience the researcher felt with her. To explain the whys, whats, and hows of the researcher in regard of their wonderful relationship. To assure the *"lablab* that she is the reason as to why the researcher found peace and true happiness after countless of failed connections. 

### 1.3.2 Specific Objectives
Specifically, this study hopefully achieved the following:

1. **To describe** the researcher's historical reliance on solitude as a means of emotional and psychological restoration.
2. **To identify** the characteristics of the relationship that contributed to the researcher's perception of emotional restoration.
3. **To compare** the researcher's perceived state before and after engaging in solitude and romantic companionship.
4. **To examine** how romantic companionship influenced the researcher's perception of rest, peace, comfort, and emotional well-being.
5. **To explore** how the relationship changed the researcher's previous understanding of solitude and social connection.
6. **To determine** whether companionship can function as a complementary or alternative form of restoration for an individual who traditionally relies on solitude. 

## 1.4 Scope and Limitations
This study was only conducted for an 11-month period focusing on the observed behaviors of the researcher and the *lablab* with only 1 sample size (of course, you're my one and only lablab), not counting the multiple personalities of Pauline May Coming Bautista. Both the researcher and the *lablab* are emotionally involved and all observations are purely subjective. The primary limitation of this study is its sample size of one (n = 1), making broad generalizations statistically questionable and emotionally irresponsible.

- Realize that sometimes, being with someone&mdash;who for them you matter&mdash;can provide you with restorative effects that no amount of solitude can give. 

## 1.6 Theoretical Framework

This study draws upon several established theoretical frameworks to explain the observed phenomenon.

**Attachment Theory** &mdash; Originally developed by Bowlby, attachment theory posits that humans have an innate need to form close emotional bonds with significant others. Secure attachment provides a "safe haven" during times of distress and a "secure base" from which to explore the world. In the context of this study, the researcher's relationship with Pauline may represent the formation of a secure attachment bond, wherein her presence functions as a safe haven that facilitates emotional restoration without the need for physical isolation.

**Social Baseline Theory** &mdash; This theory proposes that the human brain expects access to social resources and that the presence of trusted others reduces the perceived cost of navigating challenges. When a trusted companion is available, the brain essentially "offloads" some of its burden, making tasks and emotional processing less exhausting. This framework may explain why the researcher experienced less emotional exhaustion in Pauline's company despite typically finding social interaction draining.

**Self-Determination Theory** &mdash; SDT identifies three basic psychological needs: autonomy, competence, and relatedness. The researcher's preference for solitude may reflect a strong need for autonomy. However, the relationship with Pauline appears to satisfy the need for relatedness without threatening autonomy&mdash;a balance that previous relationships failed to achieve. This distinction may explain why the researcher can love deeply and still love being alone.

**Attention Restoration Theory** &mdash; Originally applied to natural environments, this theory suggests that restoration occurs in settings that provide a sense of "being away," fascination, extent, and compatibility. The researcher hypothesizes that Pauline's presence may satisfy similar conditions: being with her feels like "being away" from the demands of social performance, her personality provides gentle fascination, and their compatibility allows for effortless engagement.

## 2.0 Literature Review

### 2.1 Solitude as a Form of Restoration

Solitude, defined as the state of being alone without feeling lonely, has been widely recognized as a valuable psychological resource. Researchers have distinguished between **chosen solitude**&mdash;a deliberate withdrawal from social interaction for purposes of reflection, creativity, or restoration&mdash;and **imposed isolation**, which is associated with loneliness and negative outcomes.

For individuals who find social interaction cognitively and emotionally demanding, solitude provides an opportunity to disengage from the performance demands of social life. It is a time to stop smiling when you don't feel like smiling, to stop talking when you have nothing to say, and to simply *exist* without the weight of another person's expectations.

The researcher's experience aligns with this understanding. Solitude was not a symptom of dysfunction; it was a **strategy for survival**. It was the room with the locked door, the headphones at full volume, the unanswered messages&mdash;not out of malice, but out of necessity.

### 2.2 Social Connection and Well-Being

Despite the benefits of solitude, extensive research consistently demonstrates that social connection is one of the strongest predictors of psychological well-being. Humans are, at their core, social beings. Meaningful relationships provide emotional support, a sense of belonging, and a buffer against stress.

However, the quality of social connection matters far more than the quantity. A hundred shallow friendships cannot provide what one genuine, safe relationship can. The researcher had experienced social connections before&mdash;friendships, acquaintances, previous romantic interests&mdash;but these often felt transactional, exhausting, or conditional.

The critical question then becomes: **Is it social connection itself that restores, or is it the specific quality of the connection?**

### 2.3 Romantic Relationships and Psychological Well-Being

Romantic relationships occupy a unique position in the hierarchy of social connections. At their best, they provide a level of intimacy, vulnerability, and emotional depth that other relationships rarely achieve. Research on romantic relationships consistently identifies several factors associated with positive outcomes: **partner responsiveness**, **emotional availability**, **mutual respect**, and **perceived safety**.

At their worst, however, romantic relationships can be the most draining experience a person endures. When love is conditional, when affection is performative, when communication feels like negotiation rather than connection&mdash;the relationship becomes another source of exhaustion rather than restoration.

The researcher's previous romantic experiences fell closer to the latter category. Love was effort. Affection was obligation. Being with someone was another thing on the to-do list. This pattern reinforced the researcher's preference for solitude: **if being with someone is exhausting, why not just be alone?**

### 2.4 Emotional Safety and Restoration

Emotional safety&mdash;the perception that one can be vulnerable without fear of judgment, rejection, or punishment&mdash;is increasingly recognized as a foundational element of restorative relationships. When emotional safety is present, individuals can express their authentic selves, share their fears, and rest in the knowledge that they will not be diminished for doing so.

This concept bridges the gap between solitude and companionship. The researcher's preference for solitude may not have been a preference for *aloneness* per se, but rather a preference for **emotional safety**&mdash;which, before Pauline, was only reliably found in solitude.

If this is the case, then the central phenomenon of this study is not that companionship replaced solitude, but that **Pauline provided the emotional safety that the researcher previously could only access alone**.

### 2.5 Research Gap

Existing literature extensively examines the benefits of solitude and the benefits of social relationships as independent phenomena. Studies on introversion, attachment, and restoration typically treat solitude and companionship as opposing states on a spectrum. However, limited attention has been given to the subjective experience of an individual who historically relied heavily on solitude but subsequently experienced romantic companionship as an equally&mdash;or potentially more&mdash;restorative state.

This study attempts to address that gap through a deeply personal, qualitative exploration of one researcher's journey from solitude-dependent restoration to the discovery of restoration through romantic companionship. The researcher acknowledges that the sample size of one (n = 1) makes this arguably the least generalizable study in academic history, but maintains that some things don't need a large sample size to be true.

## 3.0 Research Methodology

### 3.1 Research Design
This study employs a qualitative autoethnographic research design, wherein the researcher serves simultaneously as the primary observer and subject of the study. Autoethnography is a research method that uses the researcher's personal experience as primary data, connecting the autobiographical to the cultural, social, and relational. In this case, the researcher uses personal experience to examine the intersection of solitude, romantic companionship, and emotional restoration.

### 3.2 Participant and Relational Context

**Researcher:** Caine Ivan Bautista&mdash;a self-described solitude enthusiast, recovering overthinker, and reluctant romantic who spent years building emotional walls only to have them dismantled by a single person.

**Primary Relational Context:** Pauline May "Lablab" Coming Bautista&mdash;the aforementioned single person. Not a research participant in the traditional sense, but rather the catalyst, the variable, the phenomenon, and, most importantly, the researcher's person.

It should be noted that Pauline did not formally consent to being the subject of a research paper. She did, however, consent to being loved unconditionally, which the researcher considers a more significant agreement &hearts;.

### 3.3 Observation Period

The observation period spans approximately 11 months as of the date of writing, divided into the following phases:

| Phase | Period | Description |
|-------|--------|-------------|
| Phase 0 | Pre-relationship | Baseline: Solitude as primary restoration |
| Phase 1 | Months 1&ndash;3 | Initial interaction and development of connection |
| Phase 2 | Months 4&ndash;6 | Established relationship and deepening trust |
| Phase 3 | Months 7&ndash;9 | Increasing emotional familiarity and comfort |
| Phase 4 | Months 10&ndash;11 | Reflection, observation, and writing of this paper |

### 3.4 Data Collection

The researcher collected data through the following methods:

1. **Personal reflection and journaling** &mdash; Internal observations recorded through memory and written reflection on emotional states before, during, and after interactions with Pauline.
2. **Conversational recall** &mdash; Recollection of significant conversations, moments, and exchanges that influenced the researcher's perception of restoration.
3. **Behavioral observation** &mdash; Self-monitoring of changes in routines, preferences, and emotional patterns over the observation period.
4. **Comparative analysis** &mdash; Subjective comparison of emotional states during periods of solitude versus periods of companionship with Pauline.

The researcher acknowledges that relying on memory and subjective experience introduces significant potential for bias. However, the researcher also acknowledges that love has always been biased, and this paper was never going to pretend otherwise.

### 3.5 Data Analysis

Data was analyzed through **thematic analysis**, wherein recurring patterns and themes were identified across the researcher's experiences. The following themes emerged during preliminary analysis:

- Emotional safety
- Comfort and ease
- Acceptance without condition
- Rest without isolation
- Vulnerability without fear
- Peace in presence
- Belonging without obligation
- Anticipation without anxiety

These themes were organized into broader categories corresponding to the research objectives and are discussed in detail in Section 4.0.

### 3.6 Ethical Considerations

The researcher recognizes several ethical considerations inherent in this study:

1. **Privacy** &mdash; Specific private conversations, intimate details, and personally identifiable information about Pauline beyond what she would be comfortable sharing have been excluded from this paper.
2. **Consent** &mdash; The researcher distinguishes between Pauline's role as the relational context of this study and a formal research participant. No claims are made about Pauline's internal experiences; all observations are strictly from the researcher's perspective.
3. **Bias** &mdash; The researcher acknowledges that affection toward the subject may introduce significant interpretive bias. The researcher is, in fact, deeply in love with the relational context of this study, and any pretense of objectivity was abandoned approximately three months into the observation period.
4. **Emotional responsibility** &mdash; The researcher is committed to representing the relationship with honesty, respect, and care, even within an academic framework.

## 4.0 Results and Discussion

### 4.1 Solitude Before the Relationship

Before the observation period, the researcher's relationship with solitude could be characterized as **dependency rather than preference**. Solitude provided:

- **Silence** &mdash; Freedom from the noise of social expectation.
- **Predictability** &mdash; The comfort of knowing exactly what would happen (nothing).
- **Autonomy** &mdash; Complete control over time, space, and emotional energy.
- **Safety** &mdash; Protection from judgment, rejection, and the exhaustion of performing for others.

The researcher's room was not merely a physical space; it was a **psychological fortress**. Within its walls, there were no obligations to smile, no pressure to speak, and no risk of disappointing someone. Solitude was not merely the absence of other people; it was the researcher's established mechanism for recovery.

However, upon deeper reflection during this study, the researcher began to question whether this was truly *restoration* or merely **avoidance**. Was solitude healing the researcher, or was it simply removing the source of the wound?

### 4.2 The Emergence of an Unexpected Source of Restoration

The introduction of Pauline into the researcher's life did not follow any expected pattern. There was no strategic approach, no deliberate pursuit, no calculated decision to open up. It simply *happened*, an *organic encounter*&mdash;in the way that the most significant things often do.

What made the initial interactions remarkable was not their intensity but their **effortlessness**. Conversation with Pauline did not require the researcher to perform. Silence with Pauline did not feel awkward. Being around her did not produce the familiar drain of social exhaustion.

The researcher noted with increasing confusion that after particularly exhausting days&mdash;days that would normally result in immediate withdrawal to solitude&mdash;talking to Pauline produced a similar, and sometimes superior, restorative effect.

This observation contradicted everything the researcher had previously understood about how he recovered from emotional and psychological fatigue.

### 4.3 Emotional Safety

Among all the themes identified in this study, **emotional safety** emerged as the most significant factor distinguishing the researcher's experience with Pauline from previous relational experiences.

Emotional safety, as experienced by the researcher, manifested in several ways:

- The freedom to be silent without the silence being interpreted as disinterest.
- The ability to express vulnerability without fear of it being weaponized.
- The comfort of knowing that imperfections would not result in withdrawal of affection.
- The assurance that presence was desired, not merely tolerated.

In previous relationships and social interactions, the researcher had learned to associate the presence of others with the need to **perform**&mdash;to be interesting, to be attentive, to be "on." With Pauline, the performance stopped. Not because the researcher stopped caring, but because **she never required a performance in the first place**.

This finding aligns with attachment theory's concept of a "safe haven" and may explain why the researcher's nervous system, which typically activated in social situations, seemed to settle in Pauline's presence.

### 4.4 The Difference Between Being Alone and Feeling Alone

Perhaps the most significant insight of this study emerged from the distinction between **being alone** and **feeling alone**.

**Being alone** refers to physical solitude&mdash;the absence of other people in one's immediate environment.

**Feeling alone** refers to emotional isolation&mdash;the perception that no one truly understands, accepts, or cares for you in a way that reaches your core.

The researcher had previously conflated these two states. Being alone *felt* safe because it removed the possibility of feeling alone *around someone else*&mdash;which is arguably worse than feeling alone by yourself.

Pauline introduced a third state that the researcher had not previously considered:

> **Being with someone and not feeling alone.**

This was, for the researcher, genuinely revolutionary. The realization that another person's presence could feel as comfortable as solitude&mdash;that companionship could exist without the weight of social performance&mdash;fundamentally altered the researcher's understanding of what he had been seeking all along.

The researcher was not seeking solitude. The researcher was seeking **safety**. Solitude simply happened to be the only place he had found it&mdash;until Pauline.

### 4.5 Rest Without Isolation

Building on the previous finding, the researcher observed a pattern that challenged his core assumption about rest:

| Scenario | Expected Outcome | Actual Outcome |
|----------|------------------|----------------|
| Exhausting day &rarr; Solitude | Restoration | Restoration |
| Exhausting day &rarr; Social interaction (general) | Further exhaustion | Further exhaustion |
| Exhausting day &rarr; Time with Pauline | Further exhaustion | **Restoration** |

The third scenario consistently produced results that contradicted the researcher's expectations. After an exhausting day, talking to Pauline, playing games with her, or even simply existing in the same virtual or physical space produced a sense of calm and recovery that the researcher had previously only associated with being alone.

The researcher hypothesizes that this phenomenon occurs because Pauline's presence satisfies the conditions of restoration identified in Attention Restoration Theory: a sense of being away from demands, gentle fascination, a feeling of extent and immersion, and compatibility between the environment and the individual's needs.

In simpler terms: being with Pauline felt like *coming home* after a long day&mdash;except the home was a person, not a place.

### 4.6 The Researcher's Changing Definition of Rest

Over the 11-month observation period, the researcher observed a gradual but fundamental shift in his definition of rest:

**Before Pauline:**
> Rest = being alone. Rest = silence. Rest = the absence of other people.

**After Pauline:**
> Rest = being somewhere&mdash;or with someone&mdash;where I don't have to perform.

This shift does not negate the value of solitude. The researcher still values and requires time alone. However, the definition of rest has expanded from a single modality (solitude) to include a second modality (companionship with Pauline) that produces comparable or superior restorative effects.

The implication is profound: **the researcher was never truly restored by solitude itself. The researcher was restored by the absence of social performance.** Solitude guaranteed that absence. Pauline provided the same guarantee while also offering warmth, connection, and the knowledge that someone genuinely cared.

### 4.7 The Lablab Effect: An Unquantifiable Variable

The researcher must acknowledge a variable that resists all attempts at measurement, categorization, or academic framing.

There exists, within the observed phenomenon, an element that cannot be explained by attachment theory, social baseline theory, self-determination theory, or any other established framework. The researcher has tentatively designated this element as **"The Lablab Effect."**

The Lablab Effect encompasses:

- The way a single message from Pauline can reverse the emotional trajectory of an entire day.
- The inexplicable comfort of hearing her voice after hours of silence.
- The phenomenon wherein the researcher, who avoids social interaction, actively *seeks* interaction with one specific person.
- The observation that time spent with Pauline does not feel like time spent at all.
- The persistent desire to share mundane, insignificant details of daily life with someone&mdash;and the even more persistent desire to hear hers.

The researcher suspects that the Lablab Effect is what other researchers, poets, philosophers, and countless ordinary people have been attempting to describe for centuries.

They call it love.

The researcher concurs.

## 5.0 Conclusion and Future Works

### 5.1 Summary of Findings

This study examined the researcher's perceived restorative effects of romantic companionship compared with solitude over an 11-month observation period. The following findings emerged:

1. **Solitude served as the researcher's primary restoration strategy** prior to the relationship, providing safety, predictability, and freedom from social performance.
2. **The relationship with Pauline introduced an alternative form of restoration** that did not replace solitude but operated alongside it.
3. **Emotional safety was identified as the critical factor** distinguishing restorative companionship from exhausting social interaction.
4. **The researcher's preference for solitude was reinterpreted** not as a preference for aloneness, but as a preference for environments free from the demands of social performance.
5. **Pauline's presence consistently produced restorative effects** comparable to, and in many cases exceeding, those of solitude.
6. **The researcher's definition of rest evolved** from "being alone" to "being somewhere I don't have to perform"&mdash;a definition that encompasses both solitude and companionship with Pauline.

### 5.2 Conclusion

The researcher began this observation expecting to determine whether love could provide the same rest as solitude. After eleven months, the question itself appears to have been incorrectly framed.

The presence of Pauline did not replace solitude; rather, it changed what the researcher understood rest to mean. It was never about being alone. It was about being safe. Solitude was safe because no one could hurt you there. But Pauline was safe because she chose not to&mdash;and the researcher trusted that choice.

Maybe the researcher never needed to choose between being alone and being loved. Maybe the researcher simply needed to find someone whose presence felt like being alone&mdash;except he wasn't alone.

And maybe&mdash;just maybe&mdash;that is what rest was supposed to feel like all along.
